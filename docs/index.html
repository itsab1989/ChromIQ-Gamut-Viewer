Pages the app saved

Every page below was written by ChromIQ Gamut Viewer itself — Save this view as a web page…, nothing edited afterwards. They are here so you can see what that button gives you before installing anything.

Eight questions about papers → A guided tour with photographs: two papers compared both ways round, a batch that jumped on a date you can look up, two shells the same size with the insides moved, and what one number hides. Every figure read off the running window.

They are not pictures. Drag to turn the shape, scroll to zoom, and click a name in the key underneath to hide or show it. The strip under the picture is yours on every page, moving or not: it starts and stops the movement, changes its speed, zooms in and out, and puts the shape back the way it opened if you have gone somewhere you did not mean to.

Open more… and there is a good deal more, sorted into five short lists rather than one long one. Each shape gets its own row — make it fainter so what is behind it shows through, draw it as a net of fine lines instead of a surface, or take the colour out of it so the other one becomes obvious. One row per name in the key, so what you press and what you read are the same thing. There are also four fixed places to look from, which matters more than it sounds: press above on two different pages and you are comparing the same angle rather than two angles neither of you chose. Whoever saves the page chooses which of these it carries — and can hand over the lot, as this one does.

Two shapes can be dissolved into each other. Under more… there are two controls that fade away the part every shape reaches, or the parts only one of them does. The first leaves you looking at nothing but the differences — which is what you want when choosing between two papers. The second leaves the part they have in common, which is what you want when the same picture has to go out on both and you need to know which colours are safe on either. At the top of the range neither changes the picture at all: the fade is carried on the colours themselves rather than by drawing the shape twice, so full strength returns the identical picture, pixel for pixel.

A see-through shape shows its own back, and that is not a fault. A closed shape drawn see-through shows its far side from the inside as well, and those faces point away from the light, so they read darker than the front. Make the shape solid, or press above, and only the front is left.

What is not happening any more is slicing. A browser blends see-through surfaces in the order it draws them rather than by which is nearer, which used to punch hard-edged triangles of the far side through the near one — the same shape torn at one angle and flawless at another. Every see-through triangle in a picture, across every shape on it, is now put in farthest-first order before each frame. Measured against a correctly blended reference at eight camera angles, two papers over each other come out at 0.0% unlike it.

One shape is never offered in grey, on purpose. Where the colour is the answer — the shape that is red for what a paper cannot reach — draining it would leave a picture still carrying a name that promises two things while showing one. That switch is not built for it at all.

On a phone or a tablet one finger turns the shape, two fingers pinch to zoom, and two fingers dragged together move it about. That last pair is worth saying out loud: the viewer that draws these shapes understands one finger and no more, so until now a phone could turn a shape and never get any closer to it. Both gestures are handled by the page itself, and the buttons are there for anyone who would rather press something. The controls lay themselves out for the screen they are on — one column on a phone, three on a laptop — and on a phone held sideways, where there is barely 390 pixels of height, the panel is capped so it cannot push the picture off the screen.

Each one is a single self-contained file of about 5 MB. The 3D viewer travels inside it, so once it has loaded it needs no internet at all — it works from a memory stick, from an email attachment, or in ten years.

Two papers, compared

A matte paper solid inside a glossy one's outline Which paper should you use? Every colour the matte one holds fits inside the glossy — all of it, with nothing sticking out — but only 77.4% of the glossy fits inside the matte, and the quarter that does not is what you would give up by choosing it. Turn it and the gap between the solid matte and the glossy cage around it is that quarter. The smaller paper is the solid one on purpose: drawn the other way round, a closed surface would sit over the cage inside it and hide it completely, at every angle. The same two papers, a room each Overlaying two shapes is the right way to see where one reaches past the other, and the wrong way to judge either on its own — the front one hides the back one. Two rooms answer the other question: what does each of these actually look like? Drag either and both follow, so you are never comparing two different angles. A slice through both, at one lightness Cut both papers at the same height and look straight down. Where two shapes are close, this is the view that separates them — a difference of a few units is obvious as a gap between two outlines and nearly invisible on a solid. Slide the cut up and down with the slider in the strip: the answer to “which one reaches further into the cyans” is different near the paper white from what it is in the shadows, and a page frozen at one height could only ever give you one of them. Every outline it can show was worked out when the page was saved and travels inside it, so it moves as fast as you can drag it. The same cut, a pane each What two rooms are to two shapes, this is to two cuts. The two panes are tied together and drawn on one shared range, so zooming or moving either moves both — which is the only way a side-by-side comparison can be trusted. Two panes free to scale themselves would draw a small gamut and a large one exactly the same size. The lightness slider drives both at once, from one list of heights, for the same reason. A paper against Adobe RGB, with every control The question most people arrive with — will the pictures people send me survive on this paper? — against the space a photographer editing for print is most likely working in. The paper is solid, Adobe RGB is the cage around it, and the cage is drawn in its own colours rather than plain grey, so you can see at a glance which part of that space the paper is sitting under. It carries every control a saved page can offer: fade any shape, put a net of fine lines over it, take the colour out of it, fade where the two agree or where they differ, hide the box, move the page through its five colourings, and set the movement going in either direction.

One device, followed through time

The pages above compare shapes. These follow one device through several profiles of it, which is a different question and one no gamut can answer: two profiles can enclose almost exactly the same shape and send the colours inside it to quite different places. The run below spans 0.42% by volume — the same shape, by any measure — while the colours inside move by ΔE 3.03.

Each page carries two lines, because they disagree by design. One is how far the device has moved altogether since the first profile; the other is how far it moved since the one before. Five steps of half a ΔE each look like nothing happening and add up to a difference that is hard to miss.

One printer, four profiles, five years A printer drifting steadily: every step about the size of the last, so it is likely to keep going at the same rate. The axis is spaced by the real dates inside the profiles rather than evenly, which matters more than it sounds — an evenly spaced axis draws a steady line through a device that was quiet for three years and then moved, and the slope is the thing people read off it. The sentence saying what the lines do not mean is saved with the graph rather than left behind in the window. The same run with one profile taken out What a run looks like when you have fewer profiles than you would like: still two lines, still an answer, drawn from three profiles instead of four. A gap makes each remaining step cover more time, which the date axis shows by drawing it wider — so the picture stays honest about how much of the movement you actually watched happen. A printer that moved all at once The other shape a run can take, and the one worth telling apart: steps of 0.18, 0.18, 5.05 and 0.17. Something happened between two of these profiles rather than creeping along, and the page names the dates it happened between — because that is a thing you can go and look up, where a trend is only something to worry about. Even steps mean it will keep creeping; one big step means it may already be over. A printer that wandered off and came back The run that looks like nothing happened and isn't. It ends ΔE 0.54 from where it started — so comparing the first profile with the last would have shown you almost nothing — but in 2021 it was ΔE 5.39 away, which is hard to miss. The cumulative line arches up and comes back while the year-on-year line stays flat, and that pair of shapes means one thing only. This is the case a trend line is most likely to be read wrongly, so the page marks the furthest point and says in words that the two ends on their own would have misled you.

The shape can say nothing while the inside says plenty. Most tools compare two profiles by how much colour each holds. Here are two that hold the same amount and are not the same at all.

The shape says nothing, the inside says plenty The same printer, five years apart. The two shapes hold 818,514 and 815,615 units of colour — 0.35% apart, which is the same size by any measure anybody would use, and by volume alone this printer has not changed. Inside those two identical shells the colours have moved by up to ΔE 3.03, which is hard to miss. The cloud is split into its seven colour families, so you can switch off everything but the blues and go and look; there is a slider to hide the colours that barely moved; and underneath, one sentence per family with the number of patches it stands on. Everything works in the page itself — no internet, nothing installed.

A picture has to be read. A sentence can be forwarded. Somebody comparing this year's profile with last year's usually has to tell a colleague or a paper supplier what changed — and what they will quote is a line of text, not a cloud. So the same answer is written out.

Which colours moved, in sentences The same first-to-last comparison as the cloud below, said in seven lines — one per colour family, each carrying how many colours it stands on, because a family of eleven and a family of a hundred and thirty-seven read exactly alike without that number. Asked for by a paper manufacturer, who named the hard part in the same breath: somebody has to draw an arbitrary line around what is a red and what is a yellow. That line cannot be removed, so the page states where it is, that this application drew it, and how many colours sat close enough to it to have been counted either side. The greys are never said to have drifted toward a colour — below a chroma of 5 a colour's hue is mostly noise, and a family it landed in would be an accident. The picture is split into those same seven families, so the key beside it is a set of switches: click blues to hide them, click again to bring them back. Hide everything but one family and you can see where inside it the printer moved — which a single cloud cannot show you, because the interesting part is buried under everything else. It works in this page, offline, with nothing installed.

A line says when. It cannot say where. Those want opposite actions: a device that has drifted evenly across every colour is a calibration job, and one that has moved only in the deep blues is a different problem with a different cause — and both draw exactly the same rising line. So the same drift is worth looking at a second way.

The same drift, drawn where it happens The first and last profile of the run above, with every colour drawn where the older one puts it and painted by how far the newer one sends it instead. Across the cube the movement runs from ΔE 0.65 to 3.03 — so this printer has not drifted evenly, which is the thing the line on page 15 cannot tell you. The colour scale is fixed rather than stretched to fit: red means the same amount here as on any other page, so two of these can be held against each other.

What one measurement says about another

What a paper can no longer reach, months later The same glossy paper measured twice, months apart, with its surface painted red where the later reading can no longer reach it. Read the red as a share of the surface, not of the gamut: it marks the measured boundary points that fall outside the other shape, and on two gamuts that graze each other a great many boundary points fall just outside while almost no volume does. Here it is 54.1% of the surface and 1.8% of the volume — two different questions, and neither answer is wrong. This is also the page where the grey switch is deliberately missing.

Handing over the controls

A page that hands over everything The same two papers, saved with every control the app can put on a page. Open more… under the picture: a speed and a sweep for each direction — widen the sweep one press past the end and it goes all the way round — four arrows to move it about, four fixed places to look from, the two controls that fade away where the shapes agree or where they differ, a row for each shape with its strength, its wires and its colour, the box and its grid, the lettering, the list of names, a switch that puts the written-out numbers away, full screen, save-as-a-picture, and a button that moves the page through five ways of colouring it — dark, light, none at all, a neutral slate and plain ink for printing. Twenty-three in all, in five named groups. You choose which of these travel each time you save, so a page for a printer can hand over the lot while one embedded in a website carries none.
