Pages the app saved

Every page below was written by ChromIQ Gamut Viewer itself — Save this view as a web page…, nothing edited afterwards. They are here so you can see what that button gives you before installing anything.

They are not pictures. Drag to turn the shape, scroll to zoom, and click a name in the key underneath to hide or show it. The strip along the bottom is yours on every page, moving or not: it starts and stops the movement, changes its speed, switches turning and tipping on and off, and puts the shape back the way it opened if you have turned it somewhere you did not mean to. Whoever saves the page chooses which of those controls it carries — and can hand over more than that, as the last one here shows.

Each one is a single self-contained file of about 5 MB. The 3D viewer travels inside it, so once it has loaded it needs no internet at all — it works from a memory stick, from an email attachment, or in ten years.

A measured paper

One paper, standing still The plain case. Every point on the surface is a patch that was printed and read by an instrument. Drag it and see the dents. The same paper, turning by itself Saved with the movement running. The controls along the bottom are yours — pause it, slow it down, or turn the tipping on. Turning and tipping, with the numbers underneath Both directions of movement at once, and the figures the window was showing written out below the shape.

Two papers, compared

A matte paper's outline around a glossy one Which paper should you use? Every colour the matte one holds fits inside the glossy — but only 76.4% of the glossy fits inside the matte, and the quarter that does not is what you would give up. Turn it and you can see exactly where the glossy one pushes out through the matte one's cage. The same two papers, a room each Overlaying two shapes is the right way to see where one reaches past the other, and the wrong way to judge either on its own — the front one hides the back one. Two rooms answer the other question: what does each of these actually look like? Drag either and both follow, so you are never comparing two different angles. A slice through both, at one lightness Cut both papers at the same height and look straight down. Where two shapes are close, this is the view that separates them — a difference of a few units is obvious as a gap between two outlines and nearly invisible on a solid. There is nothing to turn here, so this one has no controls along the bottom.

A chart, before anything has been printed

A chart in ink amounts, with a skin over what survives The three axes are the printer's own controls, which is what a chart file contains — so this needs no profile at all. The red patches are the ones a matte paper cannot reach, and they form the whole outer shell. The same chart and paper, in CIELAB Identical numbers — 222 inside, 18 on the edge, 240 outside — and a completely different picture. Two true views of one measurement.

Handing over the controls

A page that hands over everything The same two papers, saved with every control the app can put on a page. Open more… along the bottom: a speed for each direction, the box and its grid, the lettering, the list of names, and a light-or-dark switch for the page itself. You choose which of these travel each time you save, so a page for a printer can hand over the lot while one embedded in a website carries none.

Dressed differently

The same scene on a light page The window and everything it saves follow whichever you choose, so a page can match the document it is going into. The small one — 56 kB instead of 4.9 MB A measured paper saved without the viewer inside it, which it fetches instead. Ninety times smaller, and it needs the internet. Worth it for a page on a website; not for one you email.

What this is for. A picture shows somebody your angle. A page like this lets them find their own — which is what you want when you send a paper measurement to a printer, a client or a forum, and they do not have the app.
